# A $2.92-\mathrm{Gb} / \mathrm{s} / \mathrm{W}$ and $0.43-\mathrm{Gb} / \mathrm{s} / \mathrm{MG}$ Flexible and Scalable CGRA-Based Baseband Processor for Massive MIMO Detection 

Guiqiang Peng ${ }^{(1)}$, Leibo Liu ${ }^{(1)}$, Senior Member, IEEE, Sheng Zhou ${ }^{(1)}$, Member, IEEE Shouyi Yin ${ }^{(1)}$, Member, IEEE, and Shaojun Wei ${ }^{(1)}$, Fellow, IEEE


#### Abstract

Communication systems' development requires service customization in aspects, such as standards, multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) scales, and algorithms. The existing hardware designs for massive MIMO detection have difficulty in achieving both high flexibility and scalability with high hardware efficiency. This article proposes a baseband processor based on a dynamic coarse-grained reconfigurable array (CGRA) for massive MIMO detection. To efficiently support various algorithm features and requirements, three optimization techniques are proposed to achieve high flexibility and scalability. First, an on-demand matrix-vector systolic array is proposed to enable flexible and scalable matrix and vector operations, reducing memory accesses by $82 \%$. Second, distributed multiinteraction data storage is designed for flexible data access and reusability. Finally, a continuable adaptive context information format is proposed to support different bit widths, operations, and extensions of MIMO systems, reducing context information by $67 \%$. These techniques achieve the improvements of $1.33 \times$, $1.34 \times$, and $1.29 \times$ in energy efficiency and $1.21 \times, 1.18 \times$, and $1.18 \times$ in area efficiency, evaluated by removing one technique at a time from the proposed architecture. Fabricated in a $28-\mathrm{nm}$ CMOS technology, the chip achieves high flexibility and scalability in supporting various detection algorithms; various MIMO scales, such as $4 \times 4,32 \times 32$, and $128 \times 8$; and baseband processing tasks, such as filtering and fast Fourier transformation. When benchmarked on various detection algorithms, the processor achieves $\mathbf{1 . 6 4 - 2 . 9 2 - G b} / \mathrm{s} / \mathrm{W}$ energy efficiency and $0.25-0.43-\mathrm{Gb} / \mathrm{s} / \mathrm{MG}$ area efficiency, which are $2.78-28.54 \times$ and $2.05-14.43 \times$ those of state-of-the-art programmable designs, respectively. To our knowledge, this is the first flexible and scalable CGRA-based baseband processor for massive MIMO detection.

## I. Introduction

MASSIVE multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) is a key technology for future wireless communication systems, such as 5G [1]-[3]. Massive MIMO detection is one of the most critical baseband processing tasks. For the development of communication technologies, it is necessary to achieve personalized and customized services based on various standards, quality-of-service requirements, MIMO scales, and algorithms. The ability to support multiple standards and multiple protocols is one of the key considerations in hardware circuit design. Therefore, a massive MIMO detection processor needs to be flexible, to adapt to the varying environmental conditions of different scenarios and to different requirements, and scalable, for reliable and seamless integration with rapidly developing and evolving baseband processing algorithms [4]. Various research efforts have been devoted to optimizing hardware architectures for either massive MIMO or traditional MIMO detection. A number of detector implementations have been proposed based on dedicated application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs), including nonlinear detectors [5]-[10] and linear detectors [11]-[18]. These ASIC implementations generally target a single detection algorithm, and they can achieve optimal or near-optimal area and energy efficiencies for the target algorithm. However, these ASIC implementations have quite limited flexibility and scalability. Another class of detectors is those based on general architectures, such as graphics processing units (GPUs) [19], [20]. These detectors achieve high flexibility and throughput, but they suffer from significant energy and area consumption, resulting in limited energy and area efficiencies. A reconfigurable processor for massive MIMO detection is a promising solution due to its high flexibility, scalability, and energy/area efficiencies.

Many reconfigurable designs for massive MIMO detectors have been achieved based on field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs), application-specific instruction set processors (ASIPs), and digital signal processors (DSPs) [4], [5], [15], [17], [18], [21]-[24]. These designs achieve high energy and area efficiencies compared with general architectures, but they typically prevail only in one or two domains of the large design space encompassing throughput, energy, area, and flexibility. Most FPGAs provide low-level, fine-grained parallelism with a high degree of flexibility. However, the flexible logic and interconnections of FPGAs result in long reconfiguration time
and high costs; consequently, an FPGA consumes a large amount of power [25]-[27]. DSPs are easy to program and have relatively low power consumptions, and they belong to the class of instruction-driven computing architectures. In each calculation process, instructions must inevitably be loaded from the instruction memory and parsed before calculations can be performed. Therefore, DSPs are flexible but relatively inefficient [28]-[30]. Very long instruction words can reduce instruction parsing, but it is still necessary in such an architecture to repeat the loading and parsing process when the instruction functions are changed. A coarse-grained reconfigurable architecture (CGRA) [31]-[33], a computational model that is not driven by instructions, is based on a data flow graph (DFG) and is oriented toward heterogeneous spatial computing. For a given configuration, a fixed circuit is formed, enabling repeated execution and thus approaching the efficiency of an ASIC. Therefore, CGRAs offer high resource utilization and data reuse rates. Several CGRA-based detector designs have been proposed, such as the node perturbation (NP) method [34], QR decomposition [35], and the minimum meansquared error (MMSE) approach [36]. However, only a limited range of MIMO detection algorithms can be supported on these processors, and the dynamic range of the processed data is relatively fixed. Thus, the flexibility and scalability of these designs need to be improved. In addition, these processors are designed only for traditional MIMO systems, whereas massive MIMO systems introduce additional challenges for the design of reconfigurable architectures.

This article proposes a baseband processor based on a dynamic CGRA for massive MIMO detection. In this processor, reconfigurable processing element arrays (PEAs) with $4 \times 4$ customized reconfigurable processing elements (PEs) for wireless communications, single reconfigurable interconnections, an optimized shared memory (SM) system, and a hierarchical computation flow are used for the data path, and a hierarchical context controller and a prefetched context memory (CM) system are designed for the context path. To further improve flexibility and scalability with high efficiency, this article proposes the following three optimization techniques.

1) An on-demand matrix-vector systolic array (OMSA) based on PEAs is proposed, which includes five types of systolic arrays that can be dynamically configured based on context information. The OMSA can achieve various types of operations and a large number of matrix and vector operations, thus reducing memory accesses by $82 \%$ and maximizing the reuse of data.
2) A distributed multi-interaction data storage (DMDS) architecture is designed, which offers storage at three levels: the system, PEA, and PE levels. In addition, a ring architecture of memories is designed. The DMDS architecture enables flexible data access patterns and data reusability in massive MIMO systems to accelerate processing.
3) A three-level continuable adaptive context information (CACI) format is presented to enable the flexible extension of context information. The CACI format flexibly supports different bit widths, operations, iterations,
and various extensions for different MIMO systems. In addition, the context information needed in massive MIMO detection algorithms can be reduced by $67 \%$.
A prototype $28-\mathrm{nm}$ test chip has been designed, which demonstrates a performance level of $1.54 \mathrm{~Gb} / \mathrm{s}$ at 800 MHz while consuming at $521-545 \mathrm{~mW}$. The chip can be programmed for a variety of parameters; MIMO scales, such as $4 \times 4$ to $128 \times 8$; and applications, such as massive MIMO detection, filtering, and fast Fourier transformation (FFT); detection algorithms, such as Cholesky decomposition (CHD) [12], LU decomposition (LUD) [13], weighted Jacobi iteration (WeJi) [17], sphere decoding (SD) [7], and NP [34]. The implementation results demonstrate the flexibility and scalability of the architecture. In addition, the processor achieves high energy and area efficiencies compared with the state-of-the-art programmable architectures, which is important for 5 G systems [4], [37]. The proposed chip is designed for use in massive MIMO detection at the base station (BS) and for other baseband processing tasks at both the BS and mobile terminals.

This article is organized as follows. Section II analyzes the flexibility and scalability considerations. Section III describes the system architecture of the proposed processor. Section IV details the three optimization techniques. Section V presents the chip implementation and extensive measurement results. Section VI briefly introduces the software tool chains. Conclusions are drawn in Section VII.

## II. Flexibility and Scalability Considerations

In an $N_{r} \times N_{t}$ MIMO system with $N_{t}$ transmitters on the user side and $N_{r}$ antennas on the BS side, the uplink system can be modeled as follows [8]: $\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{H s}+\mathbf{n}$, where $\mathbf{H} \in \mathbb{C}^{N_{r} \times N_{t}}$ is the channel matrix, $\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{C}^{N_{t} \times 1}$ is the transmitted signal vector, $\mathbf{n} \in \mathbb{C}^{N_{r} \times 1}$ is an additive white Gaussian noise vector , and $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{C}^{N_{r} \times 1}$ is the received signal at the BS. In massive MIMO detection, the main objective is to estimate the transmitted signal s. Massive MIMO detection algorithms can be classified into two categories: nonlinear and linear. Nonlinear algorithms, such as K-best [5], SD [6], [7], expectationpropagation detection (EPD) [8], message-passing detection (MPD) [9], [10], and NP [34], [38], achieve near-optimal performance. Linear detection algorithms, such as CHD and LUD [11]-[14], Neumann series approximation (NSA) [14], Gauss-Seidel (GS) algorithms [15], [16], WeJi [17], and parallelizable Chebyshev iteration (PCI) [18], offer reduced computational complexity with near-optimal detection accuracy. The NP algorithm starts by obtaining an initial MMSE result, followed by a parallel NP scheme with the purpose of generating a list of candidate vectors around the MMSE vector. In addition, sorted QR decomposition is performed. The final detection result is obtained by applying the maximum likelihood (ML) criterion to the candidate list, i.e., finding the vector with the smallest Euclidean distance [34], [38]. This section analyzes various types of algorithms and applications to summarize the characteristics of massive MIMO detection and the flexibility and scalability considerations for the design of a reconfigurable architecture.

## A. Common Computational Patterns in Massive MIMO Detection Algorithms

Massive MIMO detection algorithms include various complex-valued matrix and vector operations, and the proportions of such operations may be large in certain algorithms. For example, matrix multiplication, inversion, and decomposition operations as well as matrix-vector multiplication operations are required in the CHD- and LUD-based MMSE algorithms [12], [13], for QR decomposition in the K-best [5] and SD [6], [7] algorithms, and in the NSA [14] and MPD [9], [10] algorithms. Different algorithms require various types of operations, such as basic addition, subtraction, multiplication, and shift operations as well as signed complex-valued addition, multiplicative accumulation, and so forth. Multiplicative accumulation accounts for a large proportion of all operations. In addition, there are different sizes of operations. For instance, the sizes of the matrices and vectors involved are different for different MIMO systems; specifically, the sizes of the channel matrix $\mathbf{H}$ and the received vector $\mathbf{y}$ are affected. For example, the CHD- and LUD-based MMSE detection algorithms presented in [12] and [13] require the computation and decomposition of the Gram matrix $\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{H}^{H} \mathbf{H}$. In addition, the decomposition results are used to achieve matrix inversion, and matrix-vector multiplication is required to calculate the matched-filter vector $\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{MF}}=\mathbf{H}^{H} \mathbf{y}$. In these calculations, many types of operations are required, most of which are signed complex-valued multiplicative and vector operations. Hence, the architecture must support operations of different types and sizes, particularly matrix and vector operations.

## B. Various Operation Bit Widths and Formats

Different MIMO systems have different detection accuracy requirements. Therefore, various bit widths are needed to support these performance requirements. For example, in the NSA method [14], the operations have the bit widths of 22, 15, 12, and 8 bits; in NP [34], the bit width of the operations is 16 bits. In addition, these algorithms require bitwise logic, such as bitwise AND, OR, and XOR operations and, therefore, also require a bit width of 1 bit. Wireless systems involve various combined vector-scalar and complex-real operations, such as those required in the GS [15], WeJi [17], and PCI [18] algorithms. These algorithms also require noncomputational operations, such as data alignment after multiplicative accumulation and during data transfers of various data widths and formats. For example, in the NP algorithm [34], complexvalued vector operations are required, such as in the calculation of the matched-filter vector, whereas the sorting and arithmetic square root operations in the QR decomposition and log-likelihood ratios require scalar operations. Hence, it is necessary to design reconfigurable computing units that can support operations of various bit widths and formats to fully exploit the computing power of the hardware resources. Additionally, efficient configuration mechanisms are expected to offload noncomputational operations, e.g., data alignment, from the processing units.

## C. Flexible and Efficient Data Access Patterns and Data Reusability

To support diverse applications and algorithms, various data formats are required, resulting in a variety of data access patterns. Massive MIMO detection algorithms involve vector, scalar, real-valued, and complex-valued data and operations; therefore, the architecture needs to efficiently support these different data formats and access patterns. To further exploit data-level parallelism, multi-subcarrier processing requires various data access patterns to allow diverse operations to be performed simultaneously on multiple subcarriers. Therefore, flexible memory access schemes are required. In addition, diverse data reuse patterns exist among massive MIMO detection algorithms. For example, in explicit algorithms, such as CHD- and LUD-based MMSE detection [12], [13], the Gram matrix computation results can be reused because of the channel hardening in massive MIMO systems. In implicit algorithms, such as PCI [18], the Gram matrix is not required, but the channel matrix $\mathbf{H}$ is reused many times. Hence, a flexible and efficient configurable on-chip memory system is required.

## III. System Architecture

## A. Overall Architecture

Fig. 1 shows the top-level architecture of the proposed processor. There are five main units, including four PEAs, embedded static random access memory (ESRAM), a host controller, SM for data storage, and CM controllers. In addition, this architecture includes common peripherals. The four $4 \times 4$ PEAs are the main computing units, which are the key components for implementing task acceleration. Each PEA can be dynamically partitioned into subarrays for computations with high algorithm-level parallelism. Each PE can be configured to execute operations of certain bit widths according to the CM in the PE. 5) Hierarchical Computation Flow: In this processor, the computation flow (see Fig. 7) is divided into three levels: the system, PEA, and PE levels. First, the host controller controls the ESRAM to transfer data to one of the SMs. After all data are ready, the start address and length of the context package are transferred to one CM. After all data and context information for the PEAs are ready, the timer is initialized, and the timer and PEAs are enabled. Each PEA reads the PEA context information to determine its overall number of iterations. Each PE in the PEA reads its corresponding context information and determines its number of iterations . Then, each PE reads the contents of the context line and performs its computation until all 16 PEs have finished computing, that is, the PEA completes one round of computation. When all four PEAs finish their iterative calculations, the timer is turned off, and the number of clock cycles is printed. The required data are moved to the SM from the PEAs, and the host controller moves the SM data into the ESRAM. Then, the correctness is verified, and the results are printed. Finally, the PEAs are turned off to reduce power consumption and prepare for the subsequent operations.

The reconfigurable processor is driven by the context data stream pipeline (CDSP). The context flows, data flows, and calculations are performed in parallel in a pipelined manner. There are five segments in the processing pipeline: fetch, decode, load data, execute, and write back. First, context information is fetched from the CM through the context loader. Then, a decoding operation is performed to analyze the context information. In accordance with the context information, the ALU's input data are loaded, and then, the calculation is performed. When the calculation is finished, the result is output to the PE, and the processing is complete. Each segment of the pipeline requires two clock cycles. Except for the ALU segment, there are two operations in each segment, such as fetching an address and the corresponding data.

## IV. Optimization Techniques

In this section, three techniques are proposed for optimizing the reconfigurable architecture for massive MIMO detection. These optimization techniques are based on the features of the algorithms as well as the flexibility and scalability requirements analyzed in Section II When achieving high flexibility and scalability, the energy and area efficiencies are also improved. ## A. OMSA

1) PEA-Based OMSA Architecture: As described in Section II, a large number of matrix and vector operations are needed in massive MIMO detection algorithms. In these calculations, different PEs need to access different addresses in the same bank during the same clock cycle. Hence, a large number of memory access conflicts may arise. To optimize the architecture for a large number of matrix and vector operations, this article proposes an OMSA architecture based on PEAs. Note that the purpose of the SM arbiter is to solve the problem of different PEs accessing the same address in the same bank during the same clock cycle. The OMSA technique is designed to resolve the conflicts among different PEs accessing different addresses in the same bank during the same clock cycle. There are five types of systolic arrays that

## B. DMDS

1) DMDS Architecture: Based on the algorithm analysis, the DMDS architecture presented in this article is separated into three levels, namely, the system, PEA, and PE levels, as shown in Fig. 10. The system-level data storage mainly corresponds to the on-chip SMs. Each SM can store inputoutput data for two PEAs. PEA-level storage is provided by the GRs. Each PEA has 16 GRs, each of which is 32 bits in size; thus, the GRs of all four PEAs have a total size of 2 kb . Each GR is mainly used to store data and parameters that the related PEA needs to write from the host controller. These data and parameters generally need to be calculated in multiple PEs. PE-level storage is provided by the LRs and SRs. Each PE has 16 internal LRs, each of which has a size of 32 bits; thus, the total size of all LRs is 4 kB . The LRs are mainly used to store intermediate data inside each PE. In each PE, there are two 64-bit SRs at Input1 and Input2 to support OMSA processing (discussed in Section IV-A). Each PEA can access two SMs. The PEAs and SMs are designed in a ring architecture, as shown in Fig. 11. Each SM contains 32 banks, each of which is accessed independently. Each bank corresponds to a separate SRAM in the physical implementation, meaning that each SM can satisfy up to 32 access requests per clock cycle.

At the same time, the different levels can support different types of data reusability. In addition, because of the ring architecture of the SMs, there is no need to switch out the context information for an entire task at once, and there is no additional data transfer time. For a $4 \times 4$ PEA, Input1 and Input2 of all PEs may require up to 16 input data to be read from the SM simultaneously. In addition, compared with the case in which each PEA separately accesses one SM, the ring architecture of SMs provides stronger data mobility. The CGRA architecture designed in [34]-[36] contains independent storage systems. ## A. Analysis of Flexibility and Scalability

1) Flexibility and Scalability in Terms of Algorithms Supported: The proposed chip can be flexibly configured to
support linear and nonlinear detection algorithms. For comparison, several typical detection algorithms are considered in Tables VI and VII, such as CHD [12], LUD [13], WeJi [17], SD [7], and NP [34]. These algorithms were selected to test the processor's support for different algorithms, parameters, and MIMO scales ( $4 \times 4$ and $128 \times 8$ ). Hence, the implementation results illustrate the flexibility and scalability of the architecture. Note that massive MIMO detectors have also been implemented based on ASICs [8]-[13]. Table VI lists the results for two of the most representative massive MIMO detectors with ASIC implementations. Compared with the ASIC-based linear [12] and nonlinear [8] detectors, the energy and area efficiencies of the proposed architecture are $10 \%-12 \%$ and $12 \%-52 \%$ as high, respectively, which are reasonable results according to [34], [39], and [40]. It is possible for CGRA implementations to achieve efficiencies closer to those of ASICs, but this comes at the expense of flexibility. Applications of CGRA designs in other fields confirm this claim [33], [41]. ASICs have certain advantages in terms of energy and area efficiencies due to their highly customized architectures and memory systems. However, such an ASIC implementation must be designed for only a single target algorithm and MIMO scale. These architectures cannot be programmed in a flexible and scalable manner. With the continuing development of communication systems, the flexibility requirements are increasing, and the use of programmable hardware has become a common trend [4]. Therefore, the reconfigurable processor proposed in this article is advantageous for the current state of the art. Typically, if a detector designed for a large-scale MIMO system is used for a relatively small-scale MIMO detection, the resource overhead will be high, and the hardware utilization will be low. By contrast, a CGRA operates by configuring PEs for specific functions and releasing unused PEs to be configured for other functions, thus permitting high hardware utilization. In addition, it is difficult to define the maximum possible scale of an MIMO system. As MIMO systems scale up, ASIC implementations will encounter scalability problems. In addition, a change in the MIMO scale may also lead to a change in algorithm. In a CGRA, the final problem of how to execute the calculations for a large-scale MIMO system, such as a $256 \times 16$ and $16 \times 16$ system, can be solved by adjusting the PE scheduling mode, that is, the flexible allocation of limited resources can be successfully achieved to complete the calculations for MIMO detection at different scales.
2) Flexibility and Scalability in Terms of the MIMO Scale: With the development of various algorithms, diverse algorithms and scales of data need to be processed. As the scale of the MIMO system increases, the proposed chip can still be used to execute detection algorithms. For example, Table VII shows that the proposed architecture can be used to efficiently implement MIMO detection algorithms of different types and scales. The MIMO sizes supported range from $4 \times 4$ to $32 \times 32$. Hence, these experimental results show that the proposed processor supports MIMO scaling. ## B. Analysis of Energy and Area Efficiencies

This section focuses on the energy and area efficiencies achieved by the proposed processor on the basis of its flexibility and scalability. Table VI shows the implementation results of the proposed chip and other state-of-the-art designs benchmarked on various algorithms with different MIMO scales. Compared with the CGRA design in [34], the proposed architecture achieves $2.78 \times$ and $2.05 \times$ higher energy and area efficiencies, respectively. Compared with the FPGA designs in [7], [15]-[17], [23], and [24], the proposed architecture achieves a $28.54 \times$ higher energy efficiency and a $4.16-14.43 \times$ higher area efficiency. Thus, the proposed architecture has advantages in both energy and area efficiencies. One reason is that the reconfigurable processor architecture proposed in this article is customized for the domain of massive MIMO signal processing, including optimized designs of the data and context paths. Another reason is the optimization techniques, i.e., the OMSA, DMDS, and CACI techniques, which are designed based on the relevant algorithm and architecture characteristics. In an FPGA, a large number of LUTs are used for programming, and flexible crossbars are used for interconnection, thereby ensuring logic and interconnection with single-bit flexibility. This will result in a large amount of context information, high power consumption, and difficulty in achieving high-speed dynamic reconfiguration [25]. ## A. Simulator

The architecture proposed in this article includes two simulators, namely, a functional simulator and a system simulator, as shown in Fig. 17. The functional simulator can simulate the functions of a PEA, including context package translation and function simulation for a single context package. Based on the specifications, the functional simulator has been built in $\mathrm{C}++$. The system simulator is a cycle-accurate simulator that can simulate the whole hardware system, including PEA, data transfer, context scheduling, and multipackage joint simulations. An elaborate performance report can be extracted from the system simulator. Moreover, power profiling is enabled based on the coprocessor characteristics.

## B. Compiler

The compiler (see Fig. 18) is designed for massive MIMO detection and baseband processing. First, the C code is input, and the tasks are divided into hardware and software components. Subsequently, the LLVM front end converts the C code into a DFG. The CGRA optimal pass optimizes the DFG for better task mapping. Then, the DFG is mapped to the CGRA architecture [45], [46]. During this process, special library files can be exploited via template matching, such as a complex matrix multiplication context file in the MIMO library. The PEA module provides the PEA patterns, such as the OMSA configurations (including full systolic arrays and snake-shaped systolic arrays) and the CDSP. The memory module provides the memory architecture patterns, such as the DMDS design (including the three-level storage architecture
and the ring architecture of SMs). In addition, there are architecture specification selections for the CGRA, which can provide mapping parameters for system elements, such as the CACI, the PEA size, and the PE functions. Finally, the tasks that need to be performed by the host controller and the CGRA are combined to form the context package [47]-[49].

## VII. Conclusion

A reconfigurable baseband processor for massive MIMO detection was fabricated in the TSMC $28-\mathrm{nm}$ CMOS technology. To achieve high flexibility, scalability, and energy and area efficiencies for the data path, $4 \times 4$ reconfigurable PEAs, domain-customized reconfigurable PEs, simplified reconfigurable interconnections, an optimized SM system, and a hierarchical computation flow are proposed; for the context path, a hierarchical context controller and a prefetched CM system are designed. To flexibly support the requirements of diverse algorithms with high energy and area efficiencies, three optimization techniques are proposed: an OMSA technique based on PEAs, a DMDS technique, and a CACI format. All these optimization techniques improve the flexibility and scalability of the processor. In addition, the implementation results in silicon show that this processor achieves high energy and area efficiencies.

Shaojun Wei (M'91-F'19) was born in Beijing, China, in 1958. He received the Ph.D. degree from the La Faculté Polytechnique de Mons, Mons, Belgium, in 1991.
He became a Professor at the Institute of Microelectronics, Tsinghua University, in 1995. His main research interests include VLSI system on chip (SoC) design, electronics design automation (EDA) methodology, and application-specific integrated circuit (ASIC) design for communications.
